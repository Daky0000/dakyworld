Nobody starts with a digital systems partner. Every business begins the same way: a cousin builds the first site, an intern sets up the Instagram, somebody's friend fixes the payment gateway that broke at Christmas. This is not a failure. It is the correct way to start, because at that size the work is small and paying a firm for it would be absurd.

The difficulty is that the arrangement does not announce when it has stopped working. It degrades. And because each individual piece still sort of functions, the business keeps going for a year or two longer than it should, paying for it in ways that never appear on an invoice.

## Six signs it has stopped working

**Nobody knows where everything is.** The domain is registered to a former employee. The hosting is on somebody's personal account. Two people have the Instagram password, neither is sure which email it is attached to. This is not an inconvenience; it is an unmanaged risk to assets the business owns.

**Fixes take days because nobody is on duty.** Your freelancer is excellent and has four other clients. When something breaks on a Friday, you are in a queue you cannot see.

**Every change is a negotiation.** A new price, a new service, a staff photo — each becomes a small project with a quote and a wait. So they do not happen, and the site slowly stops matching the business.

**The tools do not talk to each other.** Enquiries in one place, customers in another, invoices in a third, and a person in the middle retyping. Covered properly in [Five places information gets stuck between your tools](/blog/where-information-gets-stuck).

**Nobody can answer "is it working?"** How many enquiries last month, from where, how many became customers. If the answer is a shrug, decisions are being made on feel.

**The same problem keeps coming back.** Something breaks, gets patched, and breaks again a few months later. Patching treats symptoms; nobody has been paid to think about the cause.

Two or three of those is normal. Five is a business paying for a system it does not have.

## What actually changes

Bringing in an outside team is not about having someone to call. It is about three things that a series of one-off engagements structurally cannot provide.

**Continuity.** Somebody holds the whole picture — what is connected to what, why that decision was made in March, what will break if you change the CRM. That knowledge is the expensive part, and with freelancers it leaves with each of them.

**A queue with a shape.** Work is prioritised rather than requested. The difference is not administrative: when everything is a request, the loudest problem wins, and the loudest problem is almost never the most valuable one.

**Improvement between emergencies.** The critical difference. Break-fix work only happens when something is broken, which means the business is never better than working. Ongoing work means the enquiry path gets slightly better in March and slightly better again in June, and those compound.

## What it is not

Worth being blunt, because expectations are where these arrangements sour.

It is not unlimited work. Any honest arrangement has a defined monthly capacity, and anything beyond it is quoted. A provider promising unlimited anything is either charging for the worst case or planning to disappoint you.

It is not a replacement for an IT department. Systems, automation and the customer journey are a different discipline from laptops, printers, networks and device security. Be clear which you are buying.

It is not instant. The first month is largely understanding what you have. That is not slow — it is the difference between fixing things and moving them around.

## How to choose, and what to ask

Three questions tell you most of what you need:

1. **What happens in month one, specifically?** A vague answer means no method.
2. **Who owns the accounts, the domains and the source files?** The only acceptable answer is: you do. Get it in writing.
3. **What is outside the agreement?** A partner who cannot describe the boundary has not thought about it, and you will find it during your first disagreement.

> The right time to bring someone in is the month you first notice you are managing the people who manage your systems.

And if the honest answer is that you are not there yet — a good freelancer and a tidy set of accounts is genuinely the right arrangement for a business of that size — then that is the answer. Being told so is worth something too.
