A website can win a design award and lose a customer in the same afternoon. The two are judged by different people against different criteria, and only one of those people is paying you.

This is not an argument against good design. Good design is what makes a business look like it can be trusted with money, and in a market where a lot of competitors look improvised, that matters more than people admit. The argument is narrower: beauty is necessary and not sufficient, and four specific things decide the rest.

## It has to arrive

The most beautiful page in the world is a blank screen for the first four seconds. On a mid-range Android phone on a patchy connection — which is what a very large share of your visitors are actually using — a heavy site simply does not finish arriving before the person gives up.

The usual culprits are dull: an enormous hero image exported at desktop resolution, a video that autoplays, six typefaces, and a stack of third-party scripts for analytics, chat, consent and a font service, each of which is a separate journey across the network before anything is drawn.

The test is not how it feels on the office wifi on a laptop. The test is the phone, on mobile data, at the end of the day. If nobody has ever looked at the site that way, look this week.

## It has to be understood

Design can obscure as easily as it can clarify. Common ways:

- **Contrast sacrificed for mood.** Pale grey text on white photographs beautifully and cannot be read by anybody over forty, outdoors, in Ghana, at midday.
- **Navigation as a puzzle.** Clever labels. "Solutions", "Approach", "Ethos". A visitor looking for prices does not want to deduce which one of those hides them.
- **Motion that delays.** An animation that reveals text as you scroll is a decision that the reader may not read until you are ready. Do it gently or not at all.
- **The offer buried under the sentiment.** Three screens of atmosphere before anybody says what is sold.

None of these look like mistakes while you are making them. They look like taste.

## It has to lead somewhere

A conversion path is just a route from interest to action that does not require the visitor to go looking. In practice that means the next step appears at every point where someone might be convinced — after the explanation, after the proof, after the price — rather than once, at the bottom.

It also means the step is proportionate. "Book a 30-minute consultation" asks less than "Request a proposal", which asks less than "Buy". A visitor who is interested but not ready needs something to do that is not nothing. A newsletter, a guide, a WhatsApp message: some way to stay in contact that does not commit them.

## It has to be changeable

This is the one that decides whether a site is still good in two years, and it is invisible at launch.

If changing a price means emailing an agency and waiting three days, prices go stale. If adding a case study means a developer, case studies stop being added. If the person who built it has moved on and nobody else understands it, the whole thing calcifies until the only option left is a rebuild — which is how businesses end up paying for the same website three times in six years.

A site the team can maintain is worth more than a slightly prettier one they cannot. Ask before you commission anything: who changes a sentence, how long does it take, and what happens when that person is unavailable.

> A website you cannot change is a website that is already out of date. It just has not noticed yet.

## How to tell which one you have

Four questions, and you can answer them in an afternoon:

1. Open the site on a phone, on mobile data. How long before you can read something?
2. Show the top of the homepage to somebody outside the business for five seconds. Ask what the company does.
3. Count the clicks from a typical landing page to making contact.
4. Ask whoever handles updates how they would change a price today.

Nothing in that list is about how the site looks. That is the point: the look is the part everyone already checks. These four are the ones that quietly decide whether the site does anything.
