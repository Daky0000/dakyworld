There is a particular kind of website that costs a lot, looks expensive, and does nothing. It has a full-screen video, a sentence about passion, a grid of icons, and somewhere near the bottom, a contact form. Everyone involved is proud of it. It produces no enquiries and nobody can say why.

The reason is almost always the same. It was built as a brochure — something to be looked at — rather than as a tool, something that does a job. A brochure's job is to exist. A tool's job is to move a visitor from not knowing you to doing something, and it either does that or it does not.

## The three questions every visitor asks

A visitor arriving cold gives you a few seconds and three questions, roughly in this order.

**What is this?** Not your values, not your journey. What do you sell, to whom, and what changes for them. If the first thing on the page could describe four different companies, you have not answered it.

**Can I trust it?** Evidence beats adjectives. A named client, a number somebody could check, a photograph of real work, a clear price. "Leading provider" is not evidence. Neither is a stock photograph of a handshake.

**What do I do now?** One obvious next step, available wherever the visitor happens to be convinced. Not a contact form in the footer of a page they will never scroll to the bottom of.

Most sites answer one of the three well, one adequately, and one not at all. Finding which one is missing is usually a ten-minute job and usually explains the silence.

## Judge it by the journey, not the page

The word "website" makes people think of pages, and thinking in pages is what produces brochures. The useful unit is the journey: the whole path from however someone found you to whatever you need them to do.

Written out, a real journey for a small business often looks like this:

1. Someone searches for a problem, or a friend sends them a link.
2. They land on a page — often not the homepage.
3. They decide within seconds whether this is for them.
4. They look for proof.
5. They look for a price, or a signal of one.
6. They do something: message, call, book, buy, or leave.
7. Somebody on your side responds — or does not, for two days.

Step seven is part of the website. It does not feel like it, because it happens in an inbox or on a phone. But from the visitor's point of view it is the same experience, and a brilliant page followed by a two-day silence performs worse than a plain page followed by a reply in ten minutes.

## The three leaks worth checking first

**The unanswered price.** Businesses avoid publishing prices to keep options open. The effect on a visitor is the opposite of the intent: no price reads as expensive, or as a negotiation they do not want to have. A range, a starting figure, or even a clear explanation of what drives the price nearly always increases enquiries — and improves them, because the people who do get in touch have already accepted the ballpark.

**The form that asks too much.** Every field is a reason to stop. If you would not ask it in the first minute of a phone call, do not ask it before you have the enquiry.

**The phone that nobody watches.** In Ghana and across West Africa a great many real conversations start on WhatsApp, not email. If your site offers only a form, you have chosen the channel your visitor is least likely to use. If it offers WhatsApp but nobody is responsible for the account, you have built a faster route to being ignored.

## What "earning its keep" means in practice

A website that works as a tool can be described in numbers by the people who run the business. How many enquiries last month. How many were the kind you want. How long until somebody replied. How many turned into work. If nobody can answer those, the site is not being managed — it is being admired.

> You do not need more traffic until the traffic you have is being handled properly. Fixing the second thing is usually cheaper than buying the first.

## It is never finished, and that is the point

The most expensive habit in this whole area is treating a website as a project that ends. A business changes — the offer sharpens, the best customers turn out to be a different group than expected, a new service outgrows the paragraph it was given. A site that is never revisited slowly stops describing the business it belongs to, and the gap widens quietly until somebody decides the only fix is another expensive rebuild.

The alternative is unglamorous. Look at what the site is being asked to do, change the part that is not working, and look again next month. That is how a website stops being a thing you paid for and becomes a thing that pays.
